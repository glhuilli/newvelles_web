Classifying 114,927 stories: four routes vs one golden set

Every story carries a 3-level classification: one of 13 major categories, one of ~60 sub-categories, and 2–6 open-vocabulary meta tags. The category list is adapted from IPTC Media Topics — the news industry's standard subject taxonomy, maintained by the International Press Telecommunications Council and used by wire agencies and newsrooms to categorize articles.

The golden set. The reference labels are a set of 4,020 stories, sampled to cover every year, every section, and both short and long-running stories, then labeled by Claude Fable 5 (a frontier model — the strongest labeler available to this project). Quality checks on that labeling: every output was valid and matched the taxonomy exactly, and only 6.4% of stories needed an "other-*" catch-all sub-category — low enough to conclude the taxonomy fits the corpus. Every route below is scored by how often it agrees with these labels.

How to read the table. n scored — golden-set stories that route labeled, and therefore the sample its scores are computed on (routes differ because of parse failures and train/test splits). Major — share of stories where the route picked the same major category as the golden set. Sub — same, for the exact sub-category. Sub | major ✓ — sub-category agreement counting only stories where the major was already right; it isolates fine-grained confusion from coarse mistakes. Tags: any overlap — share of stories where at least one of the route's tags matches a golden tag (a lenient "did it understand the story at all" check). Tags: Jaccard — overlap ÷ union of the two tag sets, averaged (a strict similarity measure; 1.0 = identical tag sets).

The ML baseline, in plain terms. Each headline is turned into a 384-number vector with MiniLM — a small sentence-embedding model that places similar sentences near each other in that vector space — and a logistic regression picks the category from the vector. Meta tags can't be handled that way (they're open vocabulary, not a fixed label list), so the baseline uses k-NN transfer: find the 5 nearest golden-set headlines in the embedding space and inherit the tags they have in common. That inherits only tags that already exist nearby — it can never write a new tag for a new story, which is why its tag scores collapse (0.20 vs 0.50 Jaccard).

What the numbers taught us. (1) Meta tags are the genuinely generative part: the LLM writes "immigration crisis" without those words appearing in the title; extractive and transfer methods can't. (2) Sub-category disagreement is often boundary fuzziness, not error: the trained model and Haiku make the same confusions (Economy↔Markets, Crime↔Politics trials). Reviewing top divergences found one systematic split — Haiku classifies by topical domain, the golden set by story function (a betting-promo roundup is shopping-deals, not football) — now codified as taxonomy v1.1 boundary rules. (3) The local 8B model fails structurally before it fails semantically: a third of its output wasn't parseable JSON, and its top "confusions" were lowercased category names. (4) A manual review of the 200 per-category top stories produced 49 label overrides (~6% of reviewed rows — consistent with 0.82 major agreement).

Ranking importance: five functions, one survivor

Candidate scores built from per-story features: days reported, distinct outlets over the story's life, total articles, and broke-to-peak distance — each era-normalized against the year's median, so pipeline growth (more feeds in 2026) can't masquerade as importance.

Selection design. The visible quality problems were never the score: an earlier per-year quota (4 × 5 years) silently consumed all 20 slots before 2026 existed, and wide spacing rules forced weak stories in. Current design: top 25 by pure F1 (7-day spacing only, to dedupe follow-ups) as the main view, with 5 per year as the guaranteed-coverage alternate — visible in both the Timeline drill (toggle beside the back button) and the Top stories tab. Landmark events are the exception: a hand-curated list of 20, matched to archive stories by search terms + date windows.

Linking stories into events: what "days of coverage" should mean

The problem. Story identity (kept deliberately unchanged) tracks one keyword-stable thread — but big events spawn a new thread per angle. October 7 shows the gap: the war was in the news 40+ consecutive days, yet it appears as 131+ separate story identities, most lasting 1–2 days; the Musk-Twitter takeover is 57 threads, including four identities carrying the same headline. Readers who lived those weeks remember continuous coverage; the identity layer can't show it.

Linking options evaluated (star-shaped around each top-25 anchor; scored by hand-checking four known events — October 7, Musk-Twitter, SVB, the Queen's death — for correct broke date, plausible duration, and leakage):

OptionRuleVerdict
Raw entity overlapshare ≥ 2 entity tokens too loose — generic tokens (biden, trump) link everything
IDF-weighted entitiesΣ idf(shared tokens) ≥ τ — rare entities count more good recall; person-entity gravity pulls in unrelated stories about the same people
Signature Jaccardkeywords ∪ entities overlap ≥ 0.15 middling on both axes
Embedding similarityMiniLM cosine to anchor headline ≥ 0.5 too narrow — one headline can't represent an event's many angles (Oct 7 found only 30 stories)
Hybrid — chosenmoderate IDF overlap AND moderate embedding similarity, OR very high IDF overlap correct broke/peak/duration on all four cases; Oct 7 → 175 stories, 124 continuous days

Definitions now used for top stories. An event's broke date = start of the coverage episode containing the peak (a stray related story days earlier can't hijack the clock). Peak = the day with the most combined articles across all linked stories. Days = the continuous run from broke until coverage disappears for more than 3 days; the full list of coverage periods (episodes) is kept and drawn in the timeline's linkage lane. Event titles are generated from the cluster's earliest and biggest member headlines — so October 7 is titled by the attack, not by whichever single angle peaked hardest.

Known limitations (v1). Linking is anchor-star, not global — only the ~300 top stories get clusters. Multi-topic digest identities can leak a stray story in (a North Korea missile brief entered the Oct 7 cluster). The peak-episode rule can date "broke" at the main run rather than a smaller earlier burst (Musk-Twitter dates from the layoffs wave, not the deal-close two weeks prior — the earlier episode remains visible in the lane). The 120-day search window caps very long events; October 7's "124 days" is a floor, not a ceiling.

Next steps

1 · A serious ML classifier. The embedding baseline was a deliberate low-effort option; its 0.78/0.63 with a frozen 90 MB model and one afternoon of work suggests real headroom. Candidates to try, roughly in order: fine-tune a modern encoder (ModernBERT or DeBERTa-v3) end-to-end on the labels instead of freezing embeddings; distill from Haiku — train on all 114,927 Haiku labels rather than 3,000 golden rows, and evaluate against the golden set (weak supervision at 30× the training data usually beats a small clean set); SetFit-style contrastive fine-tuning of the embedding model itself; larger embedding models as a drop-in check. Add abstention: below a confidence threshold, defer to the LLM — a hybrid that could match Haiku's quality at a fraction of its cost.

2 · Tags remain LLM work for now. A generative tagger small enough to run locally (fine-tuned seq2seq on Haiku's 114k tag sets) is the experiment worth running before accepting that conclusion.

3 · Event linking v2. The anchor-star linker above is v1. Remaining work: global event clustering (every story assigned to an event, not only the top ~300 anchors), learnable thresholds (fit τ against a hand-labeled set of link judgments instead of eyeballing four cases), LLM verification of borderline links (cheap Haiku yes/no on the 10–20 lowest-scoring members per cluster), and removing the 120-day window cap with an episode-following expansion. Archetypes and ranking should then move onto cluster series rather than identity series.

4 · Golden set v2. Add a second independent labeling pass (another frontier model, or human review of a slice) to measure the agreement ceiling — without it, "0.82 vs golden" can't be separated into model error vs task ambiguity. Relabel the corpus under taxonomy v1.1 boundary rules in the next full pass.
